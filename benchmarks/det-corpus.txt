The mechanical clock is a story about escapements, not about gears. An early verge escapement
let a crown wheel advance one tooth at a time, controlled by a foliot that swung with a period
set by its own inertia - and inertia is a poor regulator, sensitive to temperature, to the
angle of the case, and to how the weight is wound. The minute hand of a fourteenth-century
clock was a promise no one could keep.

The anchor escapement, usually credited to William Clement around 1670, changed the geometry:
the pendulum now swung through a small arc, roughly two degrees, and the escape wheel was
cut with teeth whose shape let it lock and release cleanly. A seconds pendulum - a rod about
a metre long - became accurate to a few seconds a day. Harrison took the same idea and removed
 its remaining dependencies: temperature compensation, a maintenance-free lubrication scheme,
and a remnant escapement so that the pendulum was never struck hard again.

def advance(wheel, teeth, pallet):
    # one tooth per half swing; the pallet geometry decides the lock
    if pallet.locked and wheel.tooth_index != pallet.next_tooth:
        raise ValueError("escape wheel slipped out of lock")
    pallet.next_tooth = (wheel.tooth_index + 1) % teeth
    pallet.locked = False
    return pallet

What matters for a historian of technology is not the date of the first clock but the date the
error budget stopped being dominated by the escapement: once friction and air drag were the
largest terms, the problem became one of materials and lubrication, and the answer stopped
being a design and started being a maintenance schedule. The long case clock, the railway
watch, and the telegraphic time signal are all consequences of that shift, and each of them
moved the cost of keeping time from the maker to the institution that had to certify it.

A short note on terminology, because it misleads: "precision" in these conversations usually
means repeatability, and "accuracy" means agreement with some external standard. A clock that
loses exactly four seconds a day is highly repeatable and badly accurate; the fix is a
regulator, not a better escapement. The pendulum's period is set by its length and by local
gravity, so a clock moved from London to the equator gains measurably - which is exactly the
kind of error that a table of corrections, and eventually a national standard of time, was
invented to absorb.
